# Strategic Navigation Tool — Concept Development Summary

---

## Executive Summary

This document captures the conceptual development of a strategic navigation tool that emerged from analysis of a novel SWOT variant incorporating decision branching and outcome mapping. Through critical assessment of validity, methodological grounding, and market gap analysis, the concept evolved into something more sophisticated and defensible than its starting point.

The core proposition is a **radar-based strategic navigation instrument** that integrates three disciplines currently operating in isolation: SWOT-based current state assessment, cone of possibilities scenario planning, and enterprise risk management. The result is a decision-support tool that tells leadership not just what their risks and capabilities are, but whether they are pointed at the right strategic destination — and which decisions will most effectively correct their bearing.

**Key findings:**

- No existing tool integrates SWOT, scenario planning, and ERM into a unified decision-support instrument
- The concept aligns naturally with COSO ERM 2017 and ISO 31000:2018, both of which mandate objective-led risk management but provide no practical tool to achieve it
- Qualitative data capture (High / Medium / Low) is sufficient and avoids the false precision that undermines analytical credibility
- The tool requires two functional layers: a radar layer for situational awareness, and a decision branch layer for route options
- Data entry is the primary integration mechanism — dependency on existing ERM system data is not realistic for most organisations
- Strategic objective setting is not optional — it defines "north" on the compass and is the foundation on which everything else depends

**The market gap is genuine.** The components of this tool exist separately — Futures Cone templates in Miro, SWOT tools as digital sticky notes, ERM platforms as compliance documentation systems — but no integrated solution exists that uses current state assessment as the anchor for a dynamic, time-aware strategic navigation instrument.

---

## 1. Starting Point: The Enhanced SWOT Model

The concept originated from a SWOT variant that added two structural elements absent from traditional frameworks: **decision branching** (SWOT items connecting forward to strategic actions) and an **outcomes column** (terminal nodes representing business results such as secured revenue, additional revenue, and stickiness).

This format had not been encountered before in integrated form, though its components are individually recognisable:

- The **TOWS Matrix** cross-references SWOT quadrants to derive strategies, but remains a static 2D grid with no flow
- **Impact Mapping** chains why → who → how → what, but starts from goals rather than current state
- **Strategy Maps** (Kaplan and Norton) link initiatives to financial outcomes hierarchically, but without lateral flow or scenario branching

The hybrid was identified as a genuine facilitation and communication tool — making the logic of a strategy visible and debatable — but with a significant validity problem: the linear causal chain from SWOT item to outcome assumes a clarity of linkage that does not exist in practice.

---

## 2. Critical Assessment: Where Linear Branching Fails

Three substantive criticisms were identified:

**False precision.** Adding probabilities to qualitative SWOT items creates an illusion of quantitative rigour. The numbers come from the most senior or confident person in the room, laundering subjective opinion through a percentage sign. This is arguably worse than leaving the assessment qualitative, because it discourages challenge.

**The linearity problem.** SWOT items are often vague. A branch from "Lack of Agentic AI capability" to "Secured Revenue" skips over multiple unstated assumptions. Probabilities cannot repair a chain that was never causally clean to begin with.

**Better tools exist for probabilistic analysis.** Decision trees, influence diagrams, and Monte Carlo models perform probabilistic decision analysis with genuine methodological foundations. The hybrid sits awkwardly between qualitative strategy tool and quantitative model, doing neither well.

**Where the concept retains value:** the flow and outcomes structure is genuinely useful as a facilitation instrument. It surfaces assumptions that normally stay implicit and makes strategic logic visible. The probabilities are the weak link; a High / Medium / Low confidence tag is more defensible than a percentage.

---

## 3. The Internal/External Linkage Problem

A more fundamental flaw in SWOT-to-outcome branching was identified: the assumption that internal factors (Strengths and Weaknesses) connect linearly to external factors (Opportunities and Threats) is structurally unsound.

The Resource-Based View of strategy (Barney, 1991) holds that sustained competitive advantage comes from building on distinctive strengths, not from patching weaknesses. Companies that attempt to be good at everything typically become excellent at nothing. The BCG portfolio logic reinforces this — dogs are not turned into stars; they are harvested and resources redirected to what already works.

The exception: a weakness demands attention only when it represents a specific vulnerability that a competitor can actively exploit. At that point it is not a weakness in isolation — it is a weakness in direct relationship to a named threat. That is a narrower and more precise claim than the general proposition that weaknesses branch to outcomes.

**The corrected framing:**

- Strengths define the *launch vector* — direction and momentum available
- Weaknesses define *cone width* — they introduce variance and uncertainty
- Opportunities define the *upper boundary* of plausible futures
- Threats define the *lower boundary* — where drift leads if vulnerabilities are exploited

In this framing, the SWOT is not a branching tree. It is the geometry of the cone itself.

---

## 4. The Cone of Possibilities

The cone of possibilities is a well-established foresight concept (originated 1988) used in scenario planning. It represents the compounding of uncertainty over time: the further from the present, the wider the range of plausible futures. The cone is honest about uncertainty in a way that probability scores are not.

**Why this has stronger validity than probability branching:**

Scenario planning does not claim to predict — it claims to *prepare*. The cone makes uncertainty explicit rather than pretending a number resolves it. Shell's use of scenario planning to anticipate the 1973 oil crisis, when no competitor did, is the canonical demonstration of its practical value.

**The SWOT image as a flattened cone:**

The original image was, in essence, a cone compressed into a single time horizon. SWOT on the left is current state. Development Focus cards in the centre are decision nodes. Outcomes on the right are one future snapshot. Stretching this across multiple time horizons — 6 months, 2 years, 5 years — produces a cone emanating from the SWOT anchor.

**Emergent context:**

New information discovered at a future reassessment does not simply add a branch — it reshapes the cone geometry. Possible effects include:

- *Widens the cone* — a new unknown introduces previously unrecognised uncertainty
- *Narrows the cone* — a constraint or commitment closes off possibilities
- *Shifts the axis* — a fundamental change means the trajectory itself was wrong
- *Invalidates the current position* — a strength proves to be a liability under new conditions

The linear branching model breaks entirely when this happens. The cone simply changes shape.

**The series of overlapping cones:**

Each reassessment creates a new cone anchored at the new present. Preserving old cones produces a track record: did outcomes fall inside the projected cone or outside it? Consistent deviation above the upper boundary suggests the initial SWOT undervalued strengths. Consistent deviation below, or outside the cone entirely, suggests weak external scanning. This embeds accountability in the model — something ERM has always struggled to achieve.

## 5. Enterprise Risk Management Integration

**The chronic failure of traditional ERM:**

ERM identifies and scores risks largely in isolation from organisational capability. A risk appears on a heat map — high likelihood, high impact — but the heat map says nothing about whether the organisation has the capability to respond. Two organisations with identical risk registers can have radically different actual exposures depending on their strengths and weaknesses. Current tools do not capture this distinction.

**How the SWOT-cone model addresses it:**

Strengths and weaknesses are not merely strategic context — they are risk response capability. In ERM terms:

- Strengths are existing controls and the capacity to absorb and exploit risk
- Weaknesses are gaps in the control environment and constraints on response options

A weakness does not connect mechanically to a threat. Rather, a weakness *determines the degree of exposure* to a specific threat. A strength does not cause an opportunity — it determines *how well-positioned the organisation is to capture it*.

**The four strategic positions this generates:**

| Risk Exposure | Response Capability | Strategic Position |
|---|---|---|
| High | High | Pursue or exploit — strengths allow aggressive action |
| High | Low | Hedge or build — invest in capability before the risk materialises |
| Low | High | Defend and extend — efficient use of strengths, risk of complacency |
| Low | Low | Avoid or exit — no vulnerability now, no ability to respond if conditions change |

This yields directional strategic outputs, not just governance documentation.

## 6. The 360-Degree Radar / Compass Rose

**The core insight:**

Every existing strategy and ERM tool measures *magnitude* — are risks higher or lower, are metrics up or down. None measure *direction*. An organisation can be moving fast in entirely the wrong direction. The 360-degree axis introduces **strategic velocity** — direction plus magnitude — which is materially more useful for decision-making.

**The compass metaphor:**

The desired strategic destination is due north. The SWOT assessment at any point produces a set of vectors — each strength, weakness, opportunity, and threat pushes with a certain force in a certain direction. The sum of those vectors is the current bearing. The gap between bearing and north is strategic drift.

**What each SWOT element contributes:**

- *Strengths* are propulsive vectors. Strengths highly aligned to the strategic objective push toward north. Strengths in irrelevant areas push east — progress, but drift.
- *Weaknesses* are drag vectors. Weaknesses aligned with live threats pull bearing southward.
- *Opportunities* are magnetic — they create pull toward a bearing, but only where capability vectors exist to respond.
- *Threats* are lateral forces — crosswinds pushing the organisation off bearing. Their deflection effect depends on capability exposure.

**The radar screen specifically:**

The radar metaphor is richer than a simple compass rose because radar conventions map directly to the strategic problem:

- Range rings → time horizons (near-term threats close to centre, long-range further out)
- Object size → impact magnitude
- Track history → where risks have been moving
- Predicted tracks → risk trajectory
- Sweep cadence → reassessment frequency
- Friend-or-foe tagging → opportunities vs. threats

Radar shows everything relative to current position and heading *simultaneously*. A risk register tells you what is out there. The radar screen tells you what is out there *relative to where you are going*.

**Emergent context on the radar:**

When a six-month reassessment surfaces something previously unknown, the radar shows immediately whether it has changed bearing or merely speed. A new threat might not slow the organisation — it might deflect it fifteen degrees east. That is qualitatively different from a risk that increases downside magnitude only. Current tools cannot distinguish between the two. The bearing plot makes it visible.

---

## 7. Alignment with COSO ERM 2017 and ISO 31000:2018

Both frameworks were updated specifically to address the same failure: risk management had become a compliance exercise disconnected from strategy. Both made objective-setting the non-negotiable foundation. Risk is only meaningful relative to objectives — precisely what "north" on the 360-degree axis represents.

**COSO ERM 2017:**

The framework's five components place Strategy and Objective-Setting second, above Performance, Review, and Reporting. Risk appetite is defined in the context of strategy, not independently. The tool replicates this sequence: define the destination, establish current position through SWOT, identify risks relative to that destination, track bearing.

In the cone model, risk appetite becomes a design parameter rather than a statement in a document. The acceptable cone width at each time horizon *is* the organisation's risk appetite, made visual. The board sets it; the tool enforces it continuously.

**ISO 31000:2018:**

The standard requires "establishing the context" before any risk identification. Its external context — market, regulatory, competitive environment — is Opportunities and Threats. Its internal context — capabilities, culture, resources — is Strengths and Weaknesses. ISO 31000 names it differently, but it is SWOT with the explicit requirement that it connects to objectives.

**The gap both frameworks leave:**

Both COSO and ISO 31000 have been widely adopted and widely criticised for the same reason: they produce governance outputs that do not change strategic behaviour. Risk registers and assurance frameworks satisfy auditors but rarely reach the room where strategic decisions are made. The bearing model changes this because it produces a single, visually intuitive output that board-level leadership can engage with directly: are we pointed at the right destination, and is that within our stated risk appetite?

## 8. Data Capture Requirements

Integration with existing ERM platforms is theoretically appealing but practically unreliable. Most organisations keep risk data in formats that are inconsistent, siloed, or politically managed. The tool requires its own simple capture mechanism.

**Three qualitative inputs are sufficient:**

1. **Objectives** — what the organisation is trying to achieve, and by when. Creates the objective hierarchy (mission → strategic objectives → operational objectives) that defines north and sets time horizons on the cone.

2. **Capability assessment against each objective** — High, Medium, or Low. Effectively captures Strengths and Weaknesses relative to what actually matters, rather than in the abstract.

3. **Risks against each objective** — likelihood and impact, both High / Medium / Low. Captures Opportunities and Threats in direct relationship to objectives, avoiding the disconnection of conventional risk registers.

Qualitative labels generate the radar picture without pretending to false precision. The tool does not require numeric scores, probability percentages, or financial impact quantification to be useful.

---

## 9. The Two-Layer Decision Model

**The radar alone is insufficient for decision-making.**

The radar provides situational awareness — current position, bearing, threat proximity, cone of possibilities. Situational awareness is necessary but not sufficient. Navigation also requires route planning: given this situation, what are the available options and where does each one lead?

**Layer 1 — The Radar (Situational Awareness):**

Shows current strategic position, bearing relative to north (the objective), proximity and direction of threats and opportunities, cone width (uncertainty), and the historical bearing track. Answers the question: *what is our strategic situation?*

**Layer 2 — Decision Branches (Route Options):**

The user defines the strategic choices under consideration — specific initiatives, investments, or moves. The tool simulates how each decision shifts the radar picture:

- Does this decision move the bearing closer to north?
- Does it route through a high-threat corridor or around it?
- Does it require capability not currently present?
- Does it widen or narrow the cone?

The original branching model from the source image becomes the decision layer sitting on top of the radar. The difference is context: decisions are not chosen between abstract branches on a diagram, but between routes on a radar screen where threats, corridors, and projected bearing for each route are simultaneously visible.

**The two layers complete each other.** The radar gives the decision branches strategic meaning. The decision branches give the radar actionability. Neither is sufficient alone.

## 10. Market Gap Analysis

| Tool Category | What Exists | What It Lacks |
|---|---|---|
| SWOT software | MindMeister, Strategyzer, ClickUp, many others | Time dimension, scenario branching, ERM integration |
| Cone / Futures tools | Miro Futures Cone template | Connection to SWOT or ERM data, decision layer |
| ERM platforms | Riskonnect, LogicGate, Archer, ServiceNow GRC | Strategic integration, visual bearing output, objective-led structure |
| Scenario planning tools | Planisware and similar | Designed for finance/portfolio teams; disconnected from SWOT; complex and expensive |

No tool uses SWOT as the anchor point for a cone emanating into time-aware scenario space, with ERM risk data mapped onto a radar and decision branches showing route options toward defined strategic objectives.

The commercial positioning is consequently distinctive: this is not another risk register tool competing with Archer or LogicGate. It is a **strategic navigation layer** — using familiar data (risk assessments, capability judgements, strategic objectives) and presenting it in a way that drives decisions, not compliance documentation.

The alignment with COSO ERM 2017 and ISO 31000 provides both regulatory relevance and a principled methodological foundation, without requiring the tool to be sold as a compliance product.

## 11. Proof of Concept Review — WEF and Shell Tier 0 Builds

Two proof-of-concept implementations were completed in June 2026: `WEF.html` (17 SDGs mapped against WEF 2026 Global Risk data) and `Shell.html` (Shell plc 9 principal risks across 5 years of Annual Reports). Neither is production software. Both are single-file HTML/Canvas implementations used to test the concept against real data.

This section records what the POCs confirmed, what they broke, and what they revealed that the concept had not anticipated.

---

### 11.1 What the POCs confirmed

**The radar metaphor works at first contact.**
Every test viewer understood the instrument within seconds without instruction. True North, bearing deviation, blip proximity, and cone width all read intuitively. The concept's central claim — that direction is more legible than a heat map — held in practice. No user asked what the numbers meant. Several asked immediately whether it was pointing in the right direction.

**Blips as navigable objects change the cognitive model.**
Positioning individual risks as objects on a screen — rather than rows in a register — produced a qualitatively different response. Viewers scanned the radar before reading any text. The spatial relationship between blips (a cyberattack blip sitting adjacent to an HSSE blip, visually proximate) surfaced causal intuition before any network diagram was opened. The concept predicted this in theory; the POCs confirmed it in practice.

**The causal network tab was used before the analysis tab.**
In the Shell build, the Risk Network tab — showing four causal chains drawn directly from Shell's own disclosed risk interdependency statements — was opened first by every reviewer. The analysis tab was secondary. This is the opposite of what a conventional risk report produces. The implication: the network is not a supporting feature. It is the primary cognitive tool. The concept document underweights this.

**History track on the outer ring is immediately read as accountability.**
The year labels collapsing consecutive same-bearing years (e.g. "22–24") on the outer ring were interpreted without explanation as "this risk has not moved in three years." That reading is directionally correct. The track creates a natural question — why has it not moved? — that a static risk register never prompts.

**Opportunities as diamonds (north-west of True North) work geometrically.**
Positioning opportunity blips west of north — pulling toward the objective rather than deflecting away from it — was understood without labelling. The visual grammar of the radar handles the threat/opportunity distinction spatially, which is more effective than colour alone.

**The blade panel tab structure (Analysis / Network / Decisions / Board Q&A) maps to a real board session flow.**
The sequence — current position, causal relationships, route options, challenge questions — mirrors how a well-run board risk discussion actually progresses. This was not designed explicitly in the concept; it emerged from implementation.

---

### 11.2 What the POCs broke or exposed

**The aggregate bearing is analytically indefensible without weighting.**
The WEF aggregate bearing (sum of 17 SDG vectors) and the Shell aggregate (sum of 9 principal risk vectors) are both unweighted. In the Shell build this was flagged explicitly as a caveat. But the single bearing number on the canvas is visually authoritative regardless of how many caveats appear in the blade panel. Users read the number first and the caveat never. An unweighted aggregate that appears precise is worse than no aggregate at all. The concept document acknowledges false precision as a problem in Section 2 but does not solve it for the aggregate bearing specifically.

**The tool mirrors its source data — it cannot challenge it.**
Both POCs were built entirely from publicly disclosed data. Shell.html uses Shell's own risk language. WEF.html uses WEF's own rankings. In both cases the tool is an elegant presentation of what the source organisation chose to say about itself. It cannot, by design, show what was omitted. The persona review against a Shell Non-Executive Director produced the sharpest finding: the tool confirms management's narrative rather than challenging it. A Board-facing tool that only reflects management input is a compliance artefact, not a strategic instrument.

**Confidence tiers (High/Medium/Low) are insufficiently prominent.**
In Shell.html, confidence tiers appear as small badges in the blade panel. On the radar canvas, a Low-confidence blip and a High-confidence blip are visually identical. This is architecturally dishonest: the tool presents uncertain inferences at the same visual weight as audited KPIs. The concept's commitment to avoiding false precision is not enforced by the visual design.

**Velocity is absent.**
The concept describes the radar as showing "strategic velocity — direction plus magnitude." Neither POC achieves this. The history track shows where a bearing has been, but not how fast it is moving. A risk that moved 12° in one year (Shell process safety events: 63 → 90 Tier 1+2 events, +43%) is rendered identically to one that has been stable for five years. Rate of change is the most decision-relevant signal in a dynamic risk environment, and it is not in either build.

**Persona mismatch: the tool speaks to the risk expert, not the decision-maker.**
The Shell build was reviewed against four personas: Non-Executive Director, Institutional Investor, Group Risk Officer, and Energy Transition Analyst. The tool served the Risk Officer most effectively — the taxonomy was right, the causal chains were real. It failed the Investor (no financial translation), the NED (mirrors management narrative), and the Analyst (no independent triangulation). The concept targets board-level leadership but the POC defaulted to risk-expert language throughout. This is a design choice that was not made consciously.

**The data entry problem is larger than anticipated.**
The concept document (Section 8) identifies data entry as "the primary integration mechanism" but treats it as a known constraint. The POC experience sharpened this considerably. Shell.html required reading approximately 400 pages of annual reports across five years to populate 12 blips, 3 issues, 4 causal chains, and 9 KRIs. That is not a data entry problem — it is a research problem. For a production tool serving a live board, this needs to be solved structurally, not managed manually.

The annual report boot sequence (identified during Shell POC review) is the most promising resolution: the Annual Report PDFs become the ingestion source rather than a manual reference. A parser — using the Claude API or equivalent — extracts the structured data model in minutes. The human review step validates and locks each extraction with its source citation. This transforms data entry from a weeks-long research task into an hour-long approval session.

**The decision layer exists but is thin.**
Section 9 of the concept describes a two-layer model: radar for situational awareness, decision branches for route planning. The Shell Decisions tab implements this in minimal form — three route options with projected bearing shifts. But the decision layer does not interact with the radar canvas. Selecting "Pursue LNG Growth" does not move any blips or redraw the cone. The decision simulation is a text panel, not a navigation instrument. The concept promises more than either POC delivers on this dimension.

### 11.3 Revised understanding of the concept

The POCs produced four revisions to the original concept:

**1. The network is the core product, not a supporting tab.**
The concept positions the radar as the primary instrument with causal relationships as context. The POC experience reverses this priority. Directors engage with causal chains first. The radar provides the spatial intuition; the network provides the reasoning. Both are necessary; neither is primary. The product architecture should treat them as co-equal layers.

**2. Independent data is a product requirement, not a future enhancement.**
A tool that only reflects management-provided data cannot serve the challenge function a board requires. Independent triangulation — analyst ratings, regulatory findings, competitor disclosures, litigation outcomes — needs to be in the data model from the start, not added later. The confidence tier system (High/Medium/Low) is the mechanism; it needs to govern visual rendering on the canvas, not just appear as a badge in a panel.

**3. Velocity requires a new data field.**
Every blip needs a `trend` field that captures rate of change, not just current position. The history track shows positions; trend shows momentum. A risk at bearing 35° that moved there from 28° in 12 months requires a different decision response than a risk that has been at 35° for five years. The field should be: `trend: 'accelerating' | 'stable' | 'decelerating'` and should be rendered on the canvas as a blip trail or directional indicator.

**4. The annual report boot sequence is the data architecture.**
The product's data layer is not a form the user fills in. It is a parser that reads the organisation's own documents and populates the data model, with a human approval step that locks each data point to its source citation. This resolves the data entry problem, preserves source traceability, and makes the confidence tier automatic (audited KPI = High; disclosed qualitative language = Medium; contextual inference = Low). This architecture was not anticipated in the original concept. It is the most significant structural finding from the POC phase.

---

### 11.4 What has not changed

The following propositions from the original concept survive the POC experience without revision:

- **True North is non-negotiable.** Without a defined strategic objective, the bearing is meaningless. Both POCs confirmed this — the tool is only coherent when the destination is explicit.
- **Qualitative inputs (H/M/L) are sufficient and more defensible than false precision.** No POC reviewer asked for percentages. Several noted the H/M/L severity language as more credible than numerical scoring.
- **The market gap is real.** Neither POC surfaced a competing tool that integrates situational awareness, causal network, and decision simulation in a single instrument. The gap identified in Section 10 remains open.
- **COSO ERM 2017 and ISO 31000 alignment is genuine.** Both frameworks mandate objective-led risk management. Both leave the same implementation gap. The concept fills it.
